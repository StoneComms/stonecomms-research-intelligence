## Header
Eyebrow: Appendix F · A newsroom check
Title: The Found Voice Test, or who is speaking here?
Standfirst: Ask these questions to see how well your story represents the person in it. They are based on academic research that coded 168 UK news articles and asked journalists why voices go missing.
Provenance: This is not a scorecard for good journalism. It is a quick way to spot where the person at the centre of the story is — and is not — being heard.

## Readout labels

Initial gloss: Answer all six to get a quick read on where the subject's voice is strong — and where it drops out.
Bench heading: Things to check
Caveat: A high score does not settle the question. Someone can be quoted and still be badly represented. This tool checks whether a voice is present, not whether the journalism is fair.
Reset: Start again
About: About this test. It is a reflection tool, not a rulebook. It comes from research into 168 UK news articles across four topics, plus a survey of 18 journalists. It has not been tested as a newsroom intervention, so it does not claim to improve coverage. Use it as a prompt before you file.

## Q1
Question: Do we hear the person at the centre in their own words?
Derivation: What the research measured: subject voice
Options: Yes — direct quote / Only through paraphrase / No — they are not heard from
Paraphrase note: You have told the reader what they think or experienced, but we do not hear them say it.
Paraphrase action: Check your notes for one short quote that sounds like them rather than you. A few words can do more than another paragraph of paraphrase.
Missing note: The story is about them, but they never speak. That may be unavoidable, but it is worth checking whether anyone actually tried to reach them.
Missing action: Check the calls, messages and emails. If there is still time, make one more attempt. If there is not, be clear in the copy about whether they declined, could not be reached, or were never available.

## Q2
Question: How soon does the reader hear them?
Derivation: What the research measured: where the voice appears
Options: In the headline or lede / In the body / Only in a caption or photo / Nowhere
Body note: They are quoted, but not early. A reader who only gets through the first few paragraphs may never meet them.
Body action: Try moving their first useful quote above the first institutional voice. If the story still works, the quote probably belongs there.
Caption note: We can see them, but we still do not hear them.
Caption action: If you have a usable quote, put it in the story. If you do not, make sure the picture is not doing the work of suggesting a voice that is not actually there.

## Q3
Question: Would they recognise the way the story describes them?
Derivation: What the research measured: framing
Options: Yes — it matches how they described themselves / Partly / I am not sure / No — the frame comes from someone else
Partly note: If you are not sure they would recognise the description, that is probably worth checking rather than guessing.
Partly action: Read the key framing line back to them, or to someone close to the situation. ‘I've written that you…’ is a perfectly normal reporting question.
No note: They may disagree with the way the story frames them. That does not automatically make the frame wrong.
No action: If the frame is justified, give them a chance to challenge it in the story. The disagreement may improve the piece.

## Q4
Question: Is somebody else doing the talking for them?
Derivation: What the research measured: official and advocate voices
Options: No — they speak for themselves / Others speak as well / Others speak instead of them
Alongside note: Other voices are useful, but check the balance. If the charity, police, government or company gets four paragraphs and the person gets one line, the story may have drifted away from its subject.
Alongside action: Count roughly how much space each voice gets. If something needs trimming, start with the intermediary rather than the person the story is about.
Instead note: The reader is getting this person's story through somebody else. Sometimes that is necessary. It is still worth noticing.
Instead action: Ask the intermediary for an introduction and try the subject directly. If that is not possible, say what is missing rather than pretending the gap is not there.

## Q5
Question: What is the tone mainly doing?
Derivation: What the research measured: tone towards the subject
Options: Helping the reader understand / Protecting the subject / Serving the story or outlet
Protect note: The piece may be going easy on the subject. Sympathy is not the same thing as fair representation.
Protect action: Would you ask the same question if the subject were a councillor, chief executive or minister? If yes, ask it here too.
Story note: The tone may be making the story more dramatic, tidy or emotionally satisfying than the reporting really supports.
Story action: Find the detail that is doing most of the emotional work. Does the reader need it to understand what happened? If not, try taking it out.

## Q6
Question: If their voice is missing, why?
Derivation: What journalists told the research
Options: Their voice is present / There was a reporting reason / It just happened / nobody questioned it
Reason note: That can be a legitimate call. Just make sure the reason is clear enough that you could explain it later.
Reason action: Put the reason in your file note. Where it helps the reader, be specific in the copy too: ‘declined to comment’ and ‘we could not reach them’ are not the same thing.
Default note: Nobody really made the call; the person simply dropped out of the reporting. That is exactly the sort of gap this test is meant to catch.
Default action: Before you file, ask one basic question: who is this story about, and did anyone try to speak to them?

## Reading outputs
Missing/displaced verdict: We hear other people, not them.
Missing/displaced gloss: The story is about this person, but their account comes through an official, advocate or other intermediary. That may be necessary, but it means the reader never gets to hear the subject challenge the version being given. In the research behind this test, that pattern was common.
Missing/unreplaced verdict: Their voice is simply missing.
Missing/unreplaced gloss: Nobody at the centre speaks, and nobody speaks on their behalf either. That leaves the framing unchallenged. Before filing, it is worth asking whether the story genuinely works without them — and why.
Ornamental verdict: They are visible, but barely heard.
Ornamental gloss: The person appears in a caption or photograph, but their own words are hard to find. A picture can make a story feel inclusive even when the subject has little real voice in it.
Protective verdict: They are heard, but handled gently.
Protective gloss: The subject has a voice, but the tone may be protecting them from questions the story would put to somebody more powerful. Sympathy can be important. It is not a substitute for treating someone as a full participant in the story.
Framed-over verdict: They are quoted, but the story is still somebody else's frame.
Framed-over gloss: Their words are in the piece, but they do not really shape how the story understands them. The useful check here is simple: could anything they said have changed your angle, or were you only looking for a quote to fit it?
High verdict: The subject has a real voice in the story.
High gloss: They speak in their own words, appear early enough to matter and are not completely spoken for by others. Good. One last check: have you also challenged them where needed, and have you missed anybody else who belongs in the story?
Middle verdict: Their voice is there, but it fades in and out.
Middle gloss: The subject is present, but not consistently. That is common under deadline. Look at the shortest bars: they show the easiest places to check before you file.
Incomplete gloss: Finish all six to get a quick read on where the subject’s voice is strong or missing.
Fallback note: Nothing obvious is flashing red here.
Fallback action: Do one last reporting check: who else belongs in this story, and have you challenged the person at the centre as well as listened to them?
