## Questions and feedback

### Q1
Question: `Is the person at the centre quoted in their own words?`
Derivation: `From: primary subject voice`
Options: `Yes, quoted directly`; `Paraphrased or described`; `Not present at all`
Paraphrase note: `You have summarised them. A paraphrase is your sentence about their life, not theirs.`
Paraphrase fix: `Go back to the notebook and find one line that is unmistakably theirs: an image, a turn of phrase, something you would not have written yourself. Six words of it will carry more than a summarising paragraph.`
Absent note: `Nobody at the centre of this speaks. Worth being honest about whether you tried and failed, or whether the story was written before you looked.`
Absent fix: `List who you called and when. If the list is short, one more attempt now is cheaper than the correction later. If the door is genuinely closed, say so in the copy: readers can tell the difference between a silence and an unanswered question.`

### Q2
Question: `Where does their voice first appear in the report?`
Derivation: `From: voice location`
Options: `Headline or lede`; `Body of the piece`; `Caption or photograph only`; `Nowhere`
Body note: `They are in there, but a reader who stops after four paragraphs never meets them.`
Body fix: `Move their first quote above the first institutional one and read it again. If nothing breaks, leave it where you put it.`
Caption note: `They are in the picture, not in the story. A reader will finish the piece unable to tell you a single thing the person said.`
Caption fix: `Find one line of speech to sit beside the photograph. If there is none to find, ask whether the caption is claiming a presence the piece has not earned.`

### Q3
Question: `Would they recognise the frame in the report?`
Derivation: `From: framing type`
Options: `Yes, it is how they described themselves`; `Partly, or I am not sure`; `No, the frame is someone else’s`
Unsure note: `If you cannot say whether they would recognise themselves, that is a phone call rather than a judgement call.`
Unsure fix: `Read the framing sentence back to them, or to someone who knows them. Starting with ‘I have written that you…’ is a fair question and takes a minute.`
No note: `You have written a frame they would argue with. That can still be the right frame.`
No fix: `If it is right, let them argue with it inside the piece rather than after it runs. One line of theirs contesting the frame costs you nothing and makes the story much harder to dismiss.`

### Q4
Question: `Do other voices stand in their place?`
Derivation: `From: official and advocate voice`
Options: `No, they speak for themselves`; `Others speak alongside them`; `Others speak instead of them`
Footer-added option: `Nobody speaks for them`
Alongside note: `Others are in there with them. If the charity gets four paragraphs and the person gets one, the piece is about the charity.`
Alongside fix: `Count the lines each voice gets. Then trim the intermediary rather than the subject: the statement will still be there tomorrow, and the person may not be.`
Instead note: `Someone is explaining this person to the reader. Remember who has a press office and who does not.`
Instead fix: `Ask the intermediary to put you in touch, and say you will wait. If you cannot get there in time, name the gap in the copy instead of filling it.`

### Q5
Question: `Whose interests are served by the article’s tone?`
Derivation: `From: tone toward subject`
Options: `The reader’s understanding`; `The subject’s comfort or reputation`; `The story’s, or the outlet’s`
Protective note: `A tone that protects someone is still a tone doing something other than reporting. In the sample behind this tool, sympathy ran highest in the strand where the victim frame was most persistent.`
Protective fix: `Ask whether you would put the same question to a councillor or a chief executive. If you would, and you have not put it here, that is the distance between representing someone and looking after them.`
Outlet note: `The tone is working for the piece rather than the person. This is where sympathy turns into consumption, and it rarely feels like a decision at the time.`
Outlet fix: `Find the one detail that is there for effect rather than for understanding, and take it out. Then see whether the story still stands up. It usually does.`

### Q6
Question: `If the voice is missing, was that a decision or a default?`
Derivation: `From: the survey of journalists`
Options: `The voice is present`; `A decision I could defend on the record`; `A default nobody questioned`
Decision note: `Good. The reason will be harder to recall in a month than it is now.`
Decision fix: `Write it in the file note. And consider putting it in the piece: ‘declined to comment’ and ‘we were unable to reach’ tell a reader two different things.`
Default note: `Nobody decided this. In the survey behind this tool, that was the commonest way a voice disappeared, and the only one that costs nothing to fix.`
Default fix: `Ask the question now, while the piece is still open: who is this story about, and did anyone call them? It is a thirty-second check that catches most of these.`

## Reading outputs

`The story speaks about them, not with them.` — `Someone else is supplying the account. That is common rather than exceptional: in the sample behind this tool, subjects were absent from roughly two thirds of the articles written about them, and where an official or an advocate spoke, the subject's own voice was usually missing rather than alongside. The danger is not that the substitute is wrong. It is that a story told entirely through intermediaries can only ever be as good as their reasons for telling it.`

`Absent, and unreplaced.` — `Nobody speaks here, and nobody speaks for them either. This was the pattern in the asylum reporting: absence without substitution, where whatever frame emerged went unchallenged because there was nothing in the piece capable of challenging it. If the story stands up without the person in it, it is worth asking what the story is actually about.`

`Present, but ornamental.` — `They appear, but not where appearance counts. Articles that carried a subject only in a caption or a photograph were coded as containing their voice, and they read as sympathetic, which is exactly the trouble. Presence of this kind creates the impression of inclusion, and even of rigour, where there is neither.`

`Protective, but not complex.` — `The voice is here and the tone is kind, which is the combination hardest to see a problem in. Yet a subject who is handled gently is rarely a subject who is asked to account for anything, and unchallenged testimony is its own way of not taking someone seriously. The coverage studied here was at its most sympathetic in the strand where people were most persistently cast as victims. Kindness and fair representation are not the same instrument, and the first can quietly stand in for the second.`

`Included, but not on their own terms.` — `The voice is in the piece; the account is not theirs. This is the harder failure because from the outside it looks like success. Including a subject's own words shifted framing and tone markedly across all four strands studied, but inclusion and fair representation are not the same thing, and a quote gathered to serve a frame that was settled before the call does not disturb that frame, however authentic the words. The question is whether the quote could have changed the story, or was only ever going to illustrate it.`

`The story has found its voice.` — `They speak, they are placed where it counts, and the account is recognisably theirs. That is rarer than it should be. Two things are still worth asking before it goes. Were they challenged, or only listened to, because unchallenged testimony is its own way of not taking someone seriously. And who else was in this story that you did not call?`

`Partly theirs.` — `They are in the story but not throughout it. Most coverage sits here rather than at either extreme, and it is usually the work of deadline rather than intent. The shortest bars above are where the piece thins out, and they are the cheapest thing to fix while it is still open.`

Incomplete gloss: `The reading appears once all six are answered.`

No-note fallback: `Nothing to add. Which is worth noticing in itself, because it is not where most pieces land.`
Fallback fix: `Who else was in this story that nobody called, and whether the person at the centre was challenged or only listened to.`
